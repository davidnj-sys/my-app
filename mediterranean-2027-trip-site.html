July 3 – 4

Rome, Before the Ship

Two nights, one real day. The early arrival is insurance against a delayed flight — sightseeing is the bonus, not the point.

Saturday · Arrival Day

Land, Settle, Stay Awake

  • Overnight flight lands FCO in the morning; clear of the airport by mid-morning
  • Pre-booked vans into the city — twelve people do not wing ground transport
  • Italy runs 6 hours ahead of New Jersey. No naps past mid-afternoon
  • Evening: an easy stroll — Pantheon, Trevi, Piazza Navona. Free, no tickets, unbeatable
Sunday · The Full Day

One Big Sight, Not Two

  • Vatican Museums & Sistine Chapel — 3–4 hours; shoulders and knees covered, enforced
  • Colosseum + Forum + Palatine — one combined ticket, 3–4 hours, almost no shade
  • Both need timed-entry tickets bought months ahead. Splitting into two groups by interest is fine
  • Evening: pack, set out passports, confirm the vans for 9 a.m.
Summer transport strikes are a real thing in Italy. They're announced days ahead and run fixed windows. We check the strike calendar on July 3–4; if one is called for the 5th, the vans leave earlier. Nobody free-lances the morning of.

09

Santorini

Fri, July 9 · Greece · tender port

The postcard island, and the most demanding logistics of the cruise. This is the day that goes wrong for groups that improvise.

  • The ship anchors in the caldera; small boats (tenders) run ashore to Skala
  • Skala to Fira: cable car, donkey path, or 588 steps on foot
  • The afternoon cable-car queue back down is the worst bottleneck of the week — an hour-plus is routine
  • Ship excursions get tender priority; independents wait
  • Oia — the village in every photo — is a bus or taxi beyond Fira. Stunning, crowded, and the famous sunset happens after we sail
Group rule for Santorini: heading down the cliff two hours before the last tender, not one. All twelve of us. No exceptions, no "one more photo."
10

Ephesus · Kuşadası

Sat, July 10 · Turkey

A Roman city of a quarter-million people, preserved to the point you walk its marble main street past a two-story library façade. Twenty minutes inland from the pier.

  • Go early. July at Ephesus clears 95°F with almost no shade
  • The Terrace Houses cost extra and earn it — mosaics and frescoes under a roof, in shade
  • House of the Virgin Mary is nearby and commonly bundled into the same tour
  • US citizens need no visa for Turkey — passport only. Currency is the lira; cards and euros work in tourist areas at mediocre rates
11

Mykonos

Sun, July 11 · Greece

Whitewashed alleys, hilltop windmills, Little Venice on the waterfront. Ships use the new port two kilometers out — take the shuttle.

  • Chora, the main town, is a deliberate maze — getting lost is the point
  • Beaches range from family-quiet to famously loud. Ask before committing the group to one
  • The meltemi wind blows hard — hats need chin straps, small boat trips get cancelled
  • Last full day: settle onboard accounts tonight, set out disembarkation bags
July 12 – 14

Two Days in Athens

The cruise ends the morning of July 12; we fly July 14. Two nights in the city means Athens gets treated like a destination instead of an exit ramp.

Disembarkation
  • Settle onboard accounts on the 11th, not the morning of
  • Labeled bags outside the cabin the night before, or carry everything off yourself
  • Piraeus to central Athens: 30–45 minutes. Pre-booked vans for twelve
Monday Afternoon · Arrival Day
  • Check in, decompress — twelve people coming off a ship deserve a slow first afternoon
  • Plaka and Monastiraki — wander the old quarter, no tickets, no schedule
  • Dinner outdoors with the lit Parthenon overhead
Tuesday · The Full Day
  • Acropolis early — gates open around 8; beat the heat and the tour buses
  • Acropolis Museum after — air-conditioned, superb, directly below the hill
  • Ancient Agora if legs allow — where Athens actually lived; Socrates argued here
  • Real shoes: the Acropolis marble is polished slick by 2,500 years of feet
Or Trade the Afternoon For
  • Cape Sounion — Temple of Poseidon on a cliff over the Aegean, ~90 minutes down the coast. The classic sunset finale, back late
  • National Archaeological Museum — the gold Mask of Agamemnon and the Antikythera mechanism
  • Or nothing at all: a rooftop pool and a long lunch is a legitimate final day
Clocks move twice. Italy is 6 hours ahead of New Jersey; Greece and Turkey are 7. The ship may keep one "ship's time" all week regardless of your phone. Every all-aboard time is ship's time — trust the clock at the gangway.
July 14: Athens airport (ATH) is ~45 minutes from the center. Twelve people, international, peak season: at the airport three hours before departure. Flight details go here once booked.
Read This One First

Documents — All Twelve of Us

Every requirement on this page applies to each traveler individually. One missing document is one person not boarding, and none of it can be fixed at the pier.

Passports

Valid Through January 2028

At least six months past our July 14 return. Renewals run months in peak season — check yours by January 2027. Minors' passports expire in five years, not ten: Blake, Chelsea and Bell's need a specific look.

ETIAS — New for This Trip

Europe's Travel Authorization

Required for Americans starting in 2027. About €20, applied online, valid three years. All twelve of us need one, kids included. Without it, the airline can deny boarding in Newark.

ETIAS scam warning. The official portal is run by the European Union and costs about €20. Sites charging $80 to "process" it are middlemen at best. We apply together through the official site once it opens — wait for the group message.
Turkey needs nothing extra. US citizens enter visa-free, and cruise passengers ashore for the day are covered regardless. Passport only for the Ephesus stop.

90°F and Bright

Packing for a Mediterranean July

Hot, dry, and windy in the islands; the ship's air conditioning runs cold. Every list below has ended someone's day when ignored.

Clothing
  • Light and loose — linen and cotton over synthetics
  • One layer for cold dining rooms and Etna's altitude
  • Two swimsuits, so one is always dry
  • Shoulders-and-knees cover for churches — the Vatican enforces it
  • One smart outfit for the dressier evenings
Feet
  • Broken-in walking shoes — the single most important item anyone packs
  • Sandals for ship and beach
  • Closed shoes for ruins and volcanic grit
  • Blister plasters. You will use them or lend them
Sun & Heat
  • High-SPF sunscreen — scarce and pricey in port shops
  • Wide-brim hat with a chin cord — the meltemi wind is serious
  • Refillable water bottle each
  • Electrolyte packets — heat exhaustion sneaks up on every age
Medications — Non-Negotiable
  • Full trip supply plus one week of buffer
  • Original labeled bottles, never a loose pill organizer
  • Carry-on only, never checked
  • A written med-and-dose list carried separately
  • Critical meds split across two bags when traveling with a partner

The ship's medical center stocks basics but cannot refill your prescriptions. Neither can a Turkish pharmacy on a Saturday.

Electrics
  • Europe runs 220–240V with round-pin Type C/F outlets — US plugs don't fit
  • Two or three adapters per household
  • Phones and laptops are dual-voltage; hair tools often are not — check the label
  • A non-surge USB hub for the cabin. Surge strips get confiscated at ship security
Money · Phones · Streets

The Practical Stuff

Money
  • Italy and Greece: euro. Turkey: lira, though cards and euros work in tourist zones
  • Cards nearly everywhere; carry €100–200 cash per household for taxis, tips, stalls
  • Withdraw at bank ATMs, not the standalone machines in tourist squares
  • Always pay in local currency — "charge in USD?" is a bad rate in a costume
  • Tell your card issuers the dates; confirm one card per household has real headroom
Phones
  • Sort international plans before leaving — confirm Italy, Greece and Turkey; Turkey isn't always included
  • eSIM data plans are usually far cheaper than carrier day passes
  • At sea there is no cell signal; ship wifi is a paid package per device
  • Download offline maps of every port before leaving home
Airplane-mode the moment you board — all twelve phones. The ship's onboard cell tower is a maritime network no day pass covers. People come home to four-figure bills from data left on overnight. Teens' phones especially: wifi on, cellular data off, all week.
Pickpockets
  • Rome, Naples and Athens have skilled, organized crews working the tourist crush
  • Crossbody bags, zipped, worn in front. Nothing in back pockets, ever
  • Passports live in the cabin safe; carry photocopies ashore
  • The distraction is the theft — a spill, a petition clipboard, a helpful stranger at the ATM
Heat
  • Ephesus, the Forum and the Acropolis are serious midday exposure with no shade
  • Drink before you're thirsty; Rome's public fountains are safe and cold
  • Hard walking in mornings and late afternoons; shade and gelato in between
  • Headache, cramps, or confusion in anyone = stop, shade, fluids, say so immediately

Twelve People, One Plan

Ground Rules

Proposed, not decreed — we'll settle these together before we fly. But some version of each is what keeps a twelve-person, three-generation trip from turning into a search party.

The Agreements
  • One shared thread for the whole party, agreed on before we fly — whatever the families already use
  • Daily check-in at breakfast: today's plan and the all-aboard time, said out loud
  • Nobody ashore alone without telling someone the plan and a return time
  • Meeting point in every port: the gangway, on the hour
  • Back aboard one hour before all-aboard. Before it, not at it
  • Split up freely. Twelve people don't need to want the same thing — ruins and beaches can happen the same day
Onboard Basics
  • Your SeaPass card is room key, gangway ID, and wallet in one
  • My Time Dining: we reserve a nightly block for the full group early in the week
  • Most nights are smart casual; expect one or two dressier evenings
  • Drink and wifi packages are cheaper bought before boarding
  • The daily program lists tomorrow's times — read it every night, carry it ashore
If someone misses the ship — it's recoverable. Go straight to the port agent (name and number printed in the daily program), who coordinates catching up at the next port. Then message the group. Nobody improvises alone.

  • Care aboard is charged to your account and claimed back through insurance
